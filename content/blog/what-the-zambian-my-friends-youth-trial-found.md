When an intervention works in one country, it cannot be assumed to work in the same way somewhere else. School systems, community expectations, language, resources, and the meaning of mental-health measures can all shape what an evaluation finds.

## The research question

This study tested whether **My FRIENDS Youth**, a cognitive-behavioural program designed to prevent anxiety and strengthen resilience, would benefit children and adolescents attending low-income schools in Zambia.

## What the study did

The cluster-randomized trial involved 75 students aged 10 to 15 across four schools. Schools—not individual students—were assigned to either the intervention or a waitlist control. The program included ten weekly group sessions and two booster sessions, with measures collected before, immediately after, and three months after the intervention.

## A deliberately mixed result

The program did not produce reductions in youth-reported anxiety, depression, or parent–child conflict. Parents did, however, report reductions in internalizing symptoms, externalizing symptoms, and attention problems, as well as more positive parent–child relationships.

Both intervention and control groups also reported lower anxiety between the post-intervention assessment and three-month follow-up. That makes it difficult to attribute the later reduction to the program alone.

## What we can learn from it

Mixed findings are informative. Different reporters may observe different kinds of change, measures may not carry identical meaning across settings, and an intervention may need deeper local adaptation before its intended effects appear.

The study therefore does not support a simple claim that the program “worked” or “did not work.” It narrows the questions for the next evaluation: which outcomes matter locally, which program elements translate well, and what should be adapted with Zambian students, families, and schools?
