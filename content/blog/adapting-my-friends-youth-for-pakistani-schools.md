Mental-health programs do not arrive in a new setting as neutral packages. Language, school routines, examples, and assumptions about how young people talk about distress all shape whether a program feels understandable and useful.

## The research question

This study asked two linked questions: could the **My FRIENDS Youth Program** be translated and culturally adapted for Urdu-speaking adolescents in Pakistan, and would the adapted program show early signs of helping adolescent girls?

The first question matters as much as the second. A technically accurate translation is not necessarily a culturally meaningful one. Adaptation is therefore part of the intervention work, not a final editorial step.

## What the study did

The pilot involved 34 adolescent girls recruited from a mainstream school and a tuition academy. It used a quasi-experimental, non-randomized design: one group received the adapted program and the other did not during the study period.

The researchers examined anxiety, emotional and behavioural difficulties, and participants’ views of the program. They used both analysis of covariance and longitudinal mixed models to consider change while accounting for individual differences.

## What the findings suggest

The adapted program was associated with reductions in generalized anxiety and emotional and behavioural problems. More than half of participants also reported learning the program skills.

The findings were not uniform across every anxiety subtype, however. That mixed pattern is important. It suggests potential benefit without implying that a small pilot has established effectiveness for all outcomes or all Pakistani adolescents.

## Why the next study matters

This work supports the cultural feasibility of the Urdu adaptation and provides a basis for a larger randomized evaluation. A larger study could test whether the pattern holds across different schools, backgrounds, and groups of young people—and which parts of the program are most useful in context.
