Young people living in foster-care and orphanage settings may face substantial mental-health pressures while having limited access to preventive support. This study examined whether a culturally adapted version of **My FRIENDS Youth** could offer useful early support in one Pakistani setting.

## The research question

The study asked whether an Urdu version of My FRIENDS Youth could reduce anxiety and behavioural difficulties while strengthening coping, self-esteem, self-concept, and prosocial behaviour among adolescent girls living in an orphanage.

## What the study did

Thirty-eight adolescent girls took part in a quasi-experimental study. Girls in an orphanage setting received the program, while girls in a community setting formed a non-equivalent comparison group. The researchers used culturally adapted Urdu outcome measures and followed the intervention group for three months.

Because allocation was not randomized and the groups came from different settings, the design cannot rule out every alternative explanation for change. It is best understood as an early efficacy study rather than a definitive test.

## What the findings suggest

The intervention group showed reductions in anxiety and behavioural problems alongside improvements in prosocial behaviour, coping skills, self-esteem, and self-concept. Improvements were also maintained at the three-month follow-up.

That breadth is encouraging, particularly in a population rarely represented in intervention research. At the same time, the small sample and non-randomized comparison mean the results should guide further testing rather than settle the question.

## Why it matters

Prevention research needs to include young people whose living circumstances may increase risk and restrict access to care. This study offers preliminary evidence that culturally adapted, skills-based support can be feasible in an orphanage setting and identifies outcomes worth testing in a larger controlled trial.
